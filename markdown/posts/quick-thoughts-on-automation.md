---
title: Quick thoughts on automation (Workflows)
description: "Lessons from running large-scale AWS Redshift workflows: design every step to succeed or roll back, not to page a human."
date: 2026-10-12
---

During my 4 years working at AWS (Redshift team), one of my main projects was to take care of the workflows we had for ControlPlane. ControlPlane was responsible for creating instances of Redshift for users after an API call, upgrading the machines, and fixing issues that could occur on the network or on the compute instances (e.g. broken disks, a data center outage). We used an internal framework built on top of [SWF](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/amazonswf/latest/developerguide/welcome.html). SWF allowed us to run our workflows in a distributed way: we split the workflow into different steps, each step could be performed on a different machine (so we could spread the load). Each step could have different outcomes that would define what the next step was. In the end, the workflow was a [DAG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_acyclic_graph) and SWF just needed to navigate it.

There are very interesting topics around this, but I would like to quickly explain the mindset behind writing those workflows. Every single step in the end had three possible states: (1) it succeeded; (2) it failed and we should rollback changes; (3) it failed in a terminal way, requiring manual action.

Because of the scale, we tried our best to always make each step end in either state (1) or (2). If a step was going to fail in a terminal way (3) 1% of the time, that would mean a hell week for whoever was on-call, given that 1% basically meant several times a day. That was the trick that took us from ~300 sev2 per day (meaning the team was paged 300 times on any given day) to fewer than 20 sev2.

When writing the steps, it is very easy to try to side with "safety" and "fail in a terminal way" if we are unsure of the state of the system. That way, no bug would be hidden in production and we would have "visibility" into the problems. That has a couple of problems: we don't need sev2 or someone paged to know there is a problem, we should have metrics and dashboards to tell us what is going on in our fleet. We have thousands of machines; we shouldn't care if one or another is failing, we should have the data to know which the real problems are that we need to fix without stopping the world. Another, subtler issue is that it's easier to assume someone will SSH to a machine, understand what is going on and fix the issue, but that is not how real life works. Maybe whoever worked on the feature is not online, so the on-call engineer needs to understand the new code, the state of the machine and debug from first principles. It can take hours. A better way of handling it is for the engineer writing the workflow to think through all possible outcomes and store previous states. Rolling back then becomes easy to automate.
